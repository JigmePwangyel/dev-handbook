Backups and Disaster Recovery

Nobody wants a backup; everybody wants a restore. A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup. This lesson covers what to protect, how often, how to store it safely, and how to choose a disaster recovery strategy that matches your budget and tolerance for downtime.

What can go wrong

  • Human error: DROP TABLE on the wrong terminal, a bad migration, terraform destroy in the wrong workspace. The most common cause.
  • Software bugs corrupting data silently over days.
  • Hardware/provider failure: disk, host, availability zone or entire region.
  • Malicious: ransomware, or an attacker with your credentials deleting data and backups.

Note that replication is not a backup. A replica faithfully copies your accidental DELETE within milliseconds.

Ask the business, not engineering, for the numbers, and show the price of each. "Zero data loss, zero downtime" costs an order of magnitude more than "up to 1 hour of data loss, 4 hours down".

The 3-2-1 rule (and the modern 3-2-1-1-0)

  • 3 copies of your data (production plus two backups),
  • 2 different storage media/systems,
  • 1 copy off-site (another region or provider),
  • 1 copy immutable or offline (object lock / WORM, so ransomware or a compromised admin cannot delete it),
  • 0 errors after verification (restore tests).
Separate the blast radius

Backups in the same account, with credentials the production system can use to delete them, are one compromise away from gone. Use a separate account/project, write-only credentials for the backup job and S3 Object Lock in compliance mode.

What to back up

AssetMethod
DatabasesLogical dumps and/or physical base backups plus WAL archive for point-in-time recovery
Uploaded filesObject storage versioning + cross-region replication
Config, IaC, codeGit (it is your backup if everything is in it)
Secrets and keysSecret manager export procedure; encryption keys especially, since encrypted backups are useless without them
Server stateDo not back up servers; rebuild them from IaC. Back up data only.

Postgres backup examples

# Logical dump: simple, portable, fine for small DBs. Restores are slow for big ones.
pg_dump --format=custom --file=app-$(date +%F).dump "$DATABASE_URL"
pg_restore --clean --if-exists --no-owner -d restore_test app-2026-10-05.dump

# Encrypt and ship off-site
pg_dump -Fc "$DATABASE_URL" \
  | age -r age1ql3z...cmac8p \
  | aws s3 cp - s3://acme-backups-eu/app/$(date +%F).dump.age

# cron: nightly at 02:30
30 2 * * * /opt/scripts/backup.sh >> /var/log/backup.log 2>&1

For larger or stricter systems use physical backups with WAL archiving (pgBackRest, WAL-G, Barman). Base backup plus continuous WAL shipping gives point-in-time recovery (PITR): restore to 14:31:59, one second before the bad deploy. Managed databases (RDS, Cloud SQL) provide this with a retention setting; check that it is on and long enough.

Schedules and retention

A typical grandfather-father-son policy: daily for 14 days, weekly for 8 weeks, monthly for 12 months. Retention should cover how long it might take you to notice corruption. Delete on schedule too: old backups are also a data-protection liability (GDPR erasure requests).

Restore testing: the part everyone skips

  1. Automate a restore into a scratch environment weekly or monthly.
  2. Run sanity queries (row counts, latest timestamps, checksums) and fail loudly if they do not match expectations.
  3. Record how long it took. That number is your real RTO, and it usually surprises people.
  4. Do a full game day at least twice a year: pretend the database is gone, follow the runbook, with someone who did not write it.
  5. Alert on backup absence, not only failure (a dead cron job sends no errors). Use a dead-man's-switch such as a healthchecks.io ping at the end of the job.
#!/usr/bin/env bash
set -euo pipefail
./backup.sh
curl -fsS -m 10 --retry 3 https://hc-ping.com/YOUR-UUID    # only reached on success

Disaster recovery strategies

AWS's classification is a handy spectrum: pay more to recover faster.

 Cost / complexity  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶
 Backup & restore   Pilot light        Warm standby        Multi-site active-active
 RTO: hours-days    RTO: tens of min    RTO: minutes        RTO: ~zero
 RPO: hours         RPO: minutes        RPO: seconds        RPO: ~zero
StrategyWhat runs in the DR regionRecoveryUse when
Backup and restoreNothing; only backups copied thereProvision infra from IaC, restore dataInternal tools, tolerant business, tight budget
Pilot lightCore data layer replicating (DB replica); app servers defined but offScale up compute, promote DB, switch DNSModerate RTO needs
Warm standbyFull but scaled-down copy, always running and testedScale up, switch trafficRevenue-critical services
Active-activeFull capacity in 2+ regions serving usersTraffic shifts automaticallyHighest availability; accept data-consistency complexity and 2x cost

Active-active is hard because of the database: you need multi-writer conflict handling (DynamoDB global tables, Spanner, CockroachDB) or careful regional partitioning. Many teams find that a well-rehearsed warm standby is the sweet spot.

Availability zones versus regions

Multi-AZ inside a region protects against a data centre failure and is cheap and mostly automatic: do this first. Multi-region protects against regional outages and is a bigger investment. Match to need; most outages are caused by bad deploys and config, which neither fixes.

Disaster recovery runbook essentials

  • Who declares a disaster, and how are people reached if Slack is down?
  • Step-by-step restore commands, tested, stored somewhere that survives the disaster (printed or in a different provider).
  • DNS TTLs low enough (60-300s) for failover to be quick.
  • Dependencies: third-party APIs, email, auth provider. Does DR work if they do too?
  • Credentials to reach DR resources that do not depend on the failed system (break-glass accounts).
  • Failback plan: moving back is its own migration.
Remember

The unit of success is a timed, successful restore performed by someone other than the author. Everything else (cron jobs, green dashboards, S3 buckets full of files) is a proxy.

Try it

  1. Write a script that dumps a local Postgres DB, encrypts it with age and restores it into a fresh container, then asserts a row count. Time it.
  2. Write down RPO and RTO targets for a service you run. Does your current setup meet them? What is the cheapest change that would?
  3. Enable S3 versioning plus Object Lock on a test bucket and try (and fail) to delete a locked object.
