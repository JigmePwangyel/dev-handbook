DevOps Culture, History and Metrics

DevOps is first a way of working and only second a toolset. Understanding the problem it grew out of explains why every tool in this handbook looks the way it does.

The problem: the wall of confusion

In traditional organisations, developers were rewarded for shipping features and operations for keeping systems stable. Those goals conflict: every change risks stability. So developers "threw code over the wall", operations resisted change with approval boards and quarterly release windows, and releases became rare, huge and terrifying. Large releases contain many changes, so failures are hard to diagnose, which reinforces the fear. A vicious cycle.

The Three Ways (from The Phoenix Project)

  1. Flow: speed work from left (idea) to right (production). Smaller batches, automation, fewer hand-offs.
  2. Feedback: fast, amplified feedback from right to left. Monitoring, tests, alerts, user data.
  3. Continual learning: experiment, tolerate failure, practise recovery.

Measuring it: DORA metrics

The DevOps Research and Assessment programme (now part of Google Cloud) has surveyed tens of thousands of teams for a decade. It found four measures that correlate with both delivery speed and organisational performance, and, crucially, speed and stability are not a trade-off: high performers have both.

MetricQuestionKind
Deployment frequencyHow often do we ship to production?Throughput
Lead time for changesCommit to running in production: how long?Throughput
Change failure rateWhat percent of deploys cause a failure needing remediation?Stability
Failed deployment recovery time (formerly MTTR)How fast do we restore service after a bad change?Stability

Recent reports also emphasise reliability and team well-being. Elite teams typically deploy on demand (many times per day), with lead times under a day, low failure rates and recovery within an hour.

Metrics are a mirror, not a target

Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure. Do not rank developers by deploy count. Use DORA to find bottlenecks in the system.

Why CI/CD adoption matters

  • Small batches reduce risk. A deploy of 3 commits is easy to reason about and revert; a deploy of 3 months of work is not.
  • Feedback in minutes. A failing test found 5 minutes after the commit costs far less than a bug found by customers weeks later.
  • Repeatability. The pipeline does the same steps every time; no forgotten step at 6 p.m. on Friday.
  • Deploys become boring. The goal is that releasing is a non-event, so teams release more, learn faster, and recover faster.
  • Auditability. Every production change maps to a commit, a review and a pipeline run.

SRE: reliability as engineering

Site Reliability Engineering, born at Google, applies software engineering to operations. If DevOps is the philosophy, SRE is one concrete implementation ("class SRE implements DevOps"). Key ideas:

  • SLI (indicator): a measurement, e.g. fraction of requests under 300 ms and non-5xx.
  • SLO (objective): the target, e.g. 99.9% over 30 days.
  • Error budget: 100% minus SLO. At 99.9% you may "spend" ~43 minutes of badness a month. Budget left? Ship faster. Budget exhausted? Freeze features and fix reliability. This turns the dev vs ops argument into data.
  • Toil: manual, repetitive, automatable work. Cap it (Google suggests under 50%) and automate the rest.
  • Blameless postmortems: ask "how did the system allow this?" not "who did this?". People who fear blame hide information.

DevSecOps: security shifted left

Security reviews at the end of a project find problems when they are most expensive to fix. DevSecOps moves checks into the pipeline so every commit is scanned:

StageCheck
CommitSecret scanning (gitleaks), pre-commit hooks
BuildDependency scanning (Dependabot, npm audit), SAST (static analysis)
PackageContainer image scanning (Trivy), SBOM, signing
DeployPolicy-as-code, least-privilege credentials
RunRuntime monitoring, patching, DAST

Platform engineering: the current trend

As toolchains grew (Kubernetes, Terraform, observability), asking every product developer to master all of it caused cognitive overload. Platform teams build an internal developer platform with "golden paths": opinionated, self-service templates ("create a service", "get a database") so developers get safe defaults without becoming infrastructure experts. The product is the platform; the customers are your developers.

Common anti-patterns

  • The DevOps team. Renaming the ops team "DevOps" and keeping the wall. DevOps is shared responsibility, not a job silo (platform/SRE teams are fine when they enable, not gatekeep).
  • Tool-first adoption. Buying Kubernetes before having automated tests.
  • Ticket-driven infrastructure. If developers wait days for a server, automate self-service.
  • Hero culture. One person who "just knows" how prod works. Write runbooks and automate.
